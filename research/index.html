Research

Massive stars make most of the elements between oxygen and iron. Modelling convection in the advanced burning stages is quite important to get the nucleosynthesis correct. Sometimes convective shells can merge and the mixing and burning timescales become equal. When this happens, the yields depend on the details of mixing which require 3D simulations to capture.

Constraining 3D simulations of massive-star shell mergers

My MSc showed how yields depend on 3D mixing physics. My PhD tests how observations can constrain which 3D scenarios are real. Explore how dynamic nucleosynthesis can inform planets interactively here.

3D hydrodynamics

Nuclear burning in PPMstar

In the final days before core collapse, the boundaries between convective burning shells set the structure that decides whether the shells merge and how the star explodes. With Praneet Pathak, we studied the bottom of the carbon shell in high-resolution 3D PPMstar simulations of a 25 M☉ star 11 days before collapse (Pathak et al., submitted).

Convection excites high-frequency internal gravity waves at the steep entropy step that 1D models place at this boundary. The waves mix entropy and species efficiently, with a diffusion coefficient of about 5–6 × 109 cm2 s−1, and erode the step by about 40% within hours. Flattening these gradients days before collapse favours oxygen-carbon shell mergers and changes the structure that sets explodability, and this physics is not in 1D stellar models. I also implemented nuclear burning in PPMstar, so that the simulations show how the hydrodynamics responds to the nuclear physics of carbon burning.

Supernova remnants

Cassiopeia A and pre-supernova 44Ti

44Ti is usually thought to be made in the explosion. I showed that an oxygen-carbon shell merger can make 44Ti before the explosion, and that across different 3D mixing scenarios the pre-explosive yield spreads by 4.8 dex. For some mixing conditions the pre-explosive production is larger than the explosive production of the models and matches the observations, while the 56Ni yield is unchanged (Issa & Herwig 2026).

Cassiopeia A is young and close enough that its 44Ti and its odd-Z elements are measured in X-rays. With Toshiki Sato, Kai Matsunaga and collaborators I use it to test whether a shell merger took place in its progenitor (Sato et al., submitted).

Galactic chemical evolution · Planets

The light odd-Z elements and 40K

Galactic chemical evolution models make too little P, Cl, K and Sc compared to what is measured in stars of the Milky Way, and shell mergers are the most promising way to make more. Across 24 mixing cases based on 3D simulations, I found that the pre-explosive [K/Fe] of a merger changes by more than two orders of magnitude and that the yield of radioactive 40K changes by more than three (Issa et al. 2026).

40K has a half-life of 1.25 Gyr and heats the interiors of rocky planets. A range this large in the initial 40K of a planet means that the early geodynamics of its mantle can differ greatly from that of Earth. I work on this with Stephen Mojzsis as a member of the Geoastronomy ERC Synergy project.

p nuclei

3D mixing matters as much as nuclear physics

The p nuclei are rare proton-rich isotopes that are produced in shell mergers by photodisintegrations. Convective mixing uncertainties non-monotonically impact their production and isotopic ratios. In 1D mixing and burning models of a 15 M☉ star with mixing taken from 3D simulations, the production changes by up to 1.8 dex between mixing cases, comparable to the effect of nuclear physics uncertainties.

Varying all photodisintegration rates of the unstable neutron-deficient isotopes from Se to Po by a factor of 10 changed the production by 0.6 to 0.8 dex. The reactions that matter most also depend on the mixing case (Issa et al. 2026).
